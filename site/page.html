Openings as Contagion

Chess is the rare setting where social contagion is fully observable: exposure is forced — you had to answer the opening at the board — and adoption is timestamped to the second, the first game you play it yourself. So does one exposure suffice, or does taking up an opening need reinforcement from several distinct opponents?

Verdict

Simple contagion. Exposure matters; reinforcement does not.

The adoption curve rises with the number of distinct opponents who played an opening against you — and that rise is almost entirely reproduced by a null model with the timestamps shuffled. The first exposure carries the whole effect.

01

The curve rises. That is the trap.

Adoption climbs from 15.9% to 22.7% as the number of distinct exposers d grows — textbook complex contagion, if you stop there. Turn on the shuffled-time null, which destroys causal ordering but preserves degree, ratings and opening popularity exactly.

02

Hold exposures fixed, vary the exposers

The decisive comparison isolates diversity from volume: among players who received exactly k exposures, does it matter whether they came from one opponent or four? Complex contagion says yes. The data says no.

03

Influence is real — it just needs only one exposure

Each exposed player is matched to an unexposed player within ±50 Elo and ±25% games played, over the same window. The difference answers "would they have adopted anyway?" — and absorbs external shocks, since a tournament broadcast lifts matched pairs alike.

Both models sit barely above chance. This is consistent with the simple-contagion verdict — if d carries no signal, adding it should not help — but it is equally consistent with the features being too weak to predict individual adoption at all. It supports nothing on its own.

A bug worth putting in the report

The first run printed “complex contagion (marginal)”. That verdict function read only the raw curve and never consulted the null model the pipeline had just computed. Because the null reproduces nearly the entire rise, the conclusion inverted once it was included.

curve.verdict() now requires three things at once — a raw rise, survival at fixed k, and growth of the excess over the null — and the pipeline computes the null before pronouncing. This is exactly the error the identification strategy was designed to catch, caught by its own null model.

06

Limitations

  1. One month, one rating band, one platform. The excess-over-null decay is the central claim and has not been replicated. A second month is the cheapest strengthening available.
  2. 6,881 players, not the 10⁵ projected. The filters compound harshly: 2.59M games lost to the Elo band applied to both players, 1.64M to time control, 224k to rare ECO codes, 65k to the ≥20-game activity floor.
  3. ECO-level granularity only. The ply-level robustness check is implemented (OPENING_GRANULARITY = "ply") but has not been run.
  4. Adoption ≠ repertoire change. A player may try an opening once experimentally; the design cannot distinguish that from genuine uptake.
